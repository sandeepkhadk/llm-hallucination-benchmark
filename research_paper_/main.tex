\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in critical applications, yet they remain prone to generating hallucinations. While recent literature proposes various detection mechanisms, evaluations predominantly focus on accuracy metrics, largely ignoring the computational overhead that dictates real-world viability. This paper addresses this gap by presenting a controlled comparative benchmark of three prominent hallucination detection paradigms: SelfCheckGPT, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) verification, and LLM-as-a-Judge. Using an open-source benchmarking harness, we evaluate these methods end-to-end on a stratified 100-prompt sample from HaluEval and TruthfulQA across two model scales (0.5B and 1.5B parameters) on a constrained 16 GB GPU. We measure time-to-first-token (TTFT) proxies, latency, throughput, peak VRAM, KV-cache footprints, and vector-store memory alongside standard accuracy metrics. The empirical results suggest that RAG verification occupies a highly favorable region of the accuracy-versus-cost Pareto frontier, achieving F1-scores comparable to SelfCheckGPT ($\approx$0.79--0.84) while exhibiting detection overhead approximately three orders of magnitude lower (16--19 ms vs. 21--37 s). Conversely, LLM-as-a-Judge demonstrates high precision but comparatively lower recall. The observed computational hierarchy (RAG $\ll$ LLM-as-a-Judge $\ll$ SelfCheckGPT) remains statistically significant and stable across the evaluated model scales. These findings offer an empirically grounded decision framework for selecting hallucination detection strategies under strict latency or memory budgets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly integral to automated code generation, legal summarization, medical question-answering, and customer-facing conversational agents. Despite their fluency, these models frequently generate factually incorrect or ungrounded statements, a phenomenon broadly classified as hallucination \cite{huang2023survey}. Hallucinations manifest in two primary forms: intrinsic, where the output contradicts the provided source context, and extrinsic, where the model introduces unverifiable claims. While benign in trivial applications, hallucinations pose severe liability risks in high-stakes domains, elevating hallucination detection to a critical deployment requirement in safety-sensitive environments.

Current detection methodologies predominantly fall into three paradigms. SelfCheckGPT \cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} employs a zero-resource, black-box approach by sampling multiple responses to assess consistency. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) verification, exemplified by FActScore \cite{min2023factscore} and RAGAS \cite{es2023ragas}, decomposes outputs into atomic claims and retrieves corroborating evidence from external corpora. Alternatively, LLM-as-a-Judge \cite{zheng2023judging} leverages a secondary, often larger, model to evaluate the factual fidelity of the generated text. 

Although these methods are rigorously evaluated for accuracy using precision, recall, and F1-scores, their computational overhead is frequently treated as a secondary consideration. Metrics such as Time-To-First-Token (TTFT), generation throughput, KV-cache memory footprint, and vector-store utilization are fundamental determinants of production viability. A detection mechanism that yields marginal accuracy improvements at the expense of substantially increased latency or VRAM consumption is generally impractical for real-time deployments. Furthermore, existing benchmarks often compute efficiency and accuracy under disparate conditions---utilizing different hardware, decoding parameters, and ground-truth labels---rendering cross-paradigm comparisons challenging.

To address this gap, this study introduces a controlled comparative benchmark that evaluates computational complexity as a primary outcome. Every paradigm is executed on identical generated outputs, judged against uniform gold labels, and constrained by the same hardware and decoding configurations. The specific contributions of this paper are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rigorous measurement methodology that establishes time complexity (estimated TTFT proxy, latency, throughput) and space complexity (peak VRAM, KV-cache size, vector-store footprint) as primary evaluation metrics, applied consistently across all three paradigms (Section III).
    \item An open-source benchmarking harness that empirically validates this methodology by executing end-to-end evaluations on a shared HaluEval/TruthfulQA dataset across two model scales (0.5B and 1.5B parameters), reporting accuracy and computational cost under strictly matched conditions (Section IV).
    \item A practical decision framework derived from the empirical Pareto frontier of accuracy versus compute, mapping specific latency and memory constraints to optimal detection strategies for practitioners (Section IV).
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related work in hallucination detection and efficient inference. Section III details the proposed methodology and experimental design. Section IV presents the empirical results and statistical analysis. Section V discusses the practical implications and limitations, followed by the conclusion in Section VI.

\section{Literature Review}

This section reviews the taxonomy of LLM hallucinations, the foundational constraints of computational efficiency in autoregressive inference, and the operational mechanics of prevailing detection paradigms.

\subsection{Hallucination in Large Language Models}

Huang et al. \cite{huang2023survey} categorize LLM hallucinations into intrinsic and extrinsic variants. Intrinsic hallucinations directly contradict the provided input context, whereas extrinsic hallucinations introduce ungrounded information that the source neither confirms nor denies. The prevalence of these errors correlates positively with generation length and task open-endedness. Consequently, effective detection mechanisms typically operate at the sequence level, which inherently increases their computational requirements. This study adopts this intrinsic/extrinsic taxonomy to construct stratified evaluation datasets (Section III).

\subsection{Computational Efficiency in LLM Inference}

Evaluating detection overhead necessitates anchoring metrics in the efficient-inference literature. Zhou et al. \cite{zhou2024efficient} establish Time-To-First-Token (TTFT) and end-to-end latency as standard temporal metrics, alongside throughput for batched workloads. Spatially, peak memory footprint dominates resource constraints. Kwon et al. \cite{kwon2023efficient} demonstrate that KV-cache allocation during autoregressive decoding introduces severe memory fragmentation, a problem exacerbated when generating multiple concurrent sequences. For retrieval-augmented pipelines, the vector-store memory footprint emerges as an independent spatial constraint, decoupled from the LLM's native VRAM requirements.

\subsection{Hallucination Detection Paradigms}

\textbf{Self-Consistency and SelfCheckGPT.} Manakul et al. \cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} proposed SelfCheckGPT, which assesses factual reliability through repeated sampling. While conceptually simple, this approach introduces substantial computational overhead. Detecting a single hallucination requires generating $N$ additional full sequences, increasing both latency and the KV-cache memory required to maintain concurrent decoding states. For extended outputs, this spatial overhead often becomes a primary bottleneck.

\textbf{RAG Verification.} Methods such as FActScore \cite{min2023factscore} and RAGAS \cite{es2023ragas} decompose generated text into atomic claims and verify them against trusted knowledge bases. The computational profile is dominated by repeated embedding generation and nearest-neighbor retrieval. While recent optimizations like Halu-J \cite{wang2024haluj} and Bi'an \cite{jiang2025bian} attempt to reduce irrelevant retrieval overhead via critique-based judges or lightweight domain-specific models, the latency of vector database queries and the persistent memory footprint of the index remain characteristic of this paradigm.

\textbf{LLM-as-a-Judge.} Zheng et al. \cite{zheng2023judging} demonstrated that capable LLMs can evaluate the factual fidelity of peer models. Applied to hallucination detection, this paradigm circumvents vector databases and repeated sampling, maintaining a low spatial footprint. However, it concentrates costs temporally: each evaluation requires a complete autoregressive forward pass through the judge model, introducing throughput constraints and elevated TTFT. Innovations like LLM-Check \cite{sriramanan2024llmcheck} attempt to bypass full forward passes by analyzing internal hidden states, reflecting ongoing efforts to mitigate these latency constraints.

\subsection{Summary and Research Gap}

Existing literature indicates that hallucination detection is generally computationally demanding, with each paradigm exhibiting distinct temporal and spatial bottlenecks: KV-cache proliferation for SelfCheckGPT, persistent vector-store memory for RAG, and autoregressive latency for LLM-as-a-Judge. Although recent studies propose localized optimizations, no unified empirical baseline evaluates these paradigms under strictly controlled, cross-comparable conditions. Current surveys catalog accuracy but omit critical efficiency metrics, while efficient-inference tooling is rarely applied to detection algorithms. This paper bridges this gap by executing all three paradigms within a shared measurement protocol, treating computational overhead as a primary evaluation outcome rather than a secondary footnote.

\section{Methodology}

This section details the theoretical foundation, experimental design, gold-label construction, retrieval corpus, prompt standardization, data collection procedure, and analytical tools used to benchmark the computational and accuracy trade-offs of the three hallucination detection paradigms, as implemented in the accompanying open-source benchmarking harness.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework and Justification}

Our theoretical framework posits that the computational overhead of hallucination detection is not merely an implementation detail, but an algorithmic property that dictates real-world viability. We model the cost of each paradigm through the lens of efficient inference literature \cite{zhou2024efficient, kwon2023efficient}. Throughout, we are careful to separate the \emph{theoretical (asymptotic) complexity} of a paradigm from its \emph{implementation complexity} (the concrete algorithm and data structure a given library uses), its \emph{observed runtime} (wall-clock behavior on our specific hardware and software stack), and its \emph{memory consumption} (measured or estimated peak resource usage); Section IV reports the latter two as empirical measurements, while this subsection is concerned only with the former two.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SelfCheckGPT (Sampling Overhead):} The time complexity scales as $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot L)$, where $N$ is the number of sampled sequences and $L$ is the sequence length. The space complexity is dominated by the KV-cache, which must retain the key and value states for all $N$ concurrent sequences during decoding, leading to a memory footprint of $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot L \cdot d_{model})$.
    \item \textbf{RAG Verification (Retrieval Overhead):} We decompose this paradigm's cost into four distinct stages rather than a single term. (1)~\emph{Embedding generation:} encoding $C$ atomic claims costs $\mathcal{O}(C)$ forward passes through the embedding model (each itself a fixed-cost operation for a given embedding model and sequence length). (2)~\emph{Vector indexing:} building a FAISS \texttt{IndexFlatIP} index over $V$ passages costs $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d_{embed})$ to store and, if built once, $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d_{embed})$ time to populate; this cost is amortized across all queries in our fixed-corpus setting (Section III-D). (3)~\emph{Retrieval:} \texttt{IndexFlatIP} performs an \emph{exact, exhaustive} nearest-neighbor search---it computes the inner product between each query embedding and every one of the $V$ indexed vectors---so its search complexity is $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d_{embed})$ per query (equivalently $\mathcal{O}(C \cdot V \cdot d_{embed})$ for all $C$ claims in a response), \emph{not} logarithmic in $V$. This is distinct from approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indexes such as HNSW, IVF, IVF-PQ, ScaNN, or Annoy, which trade exactness for expected sub-linear (e.g., poly-logarithmic) query time by searching only a subset of the index depending on the indexing strategy and its tuning parameters; because our harness uses \texttt{IndexFlatIP} rather than any of these ANN structures, no logarithmic or sub-linear retrieval complexity applies to the results in Section IV. (4)~\emph{Verification:} comparing each claim against its retrieved evidence is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per claim given the retrieved passages, so overall verification cost is $\mathcal{O}(C)$. No reranking stage is used in the evaluated pipeline. The space complexity is decoupled from the LLM's VRAM and is instead dominated by the vector-store memory footprint, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d_{embed})$, where $d_{embed}$ is the embedding dimension.
    \item \textbf{LLM-as-a-Judge (Forward Pass Overhead):} The time complexity is characterized by a full autoregressive forward pass of the judge model, substantially impacting first-token latency and overall throughput. The space complexity is relatively predictable, requiring only the standard KV-cache for a single sequence, but at the cost of loading a second model's weights into VRAM if not shared with the generator.
\end{itemize}

This framework motivates the selection of metrics: an estimated TTFT proxy (Section III-B), throughput, peak VRAM, KV-cache utilization, and vector-store memory are the metrics we use to approximate these theoretical bounds empirically; Section IV-D discusses the extent to which the observed cost ordering is consistent with this theoretical framework, and the limits on what a benchmark of this scope can establish about asymptotic behavior.

\subsection{Research Design}

We employ a controlled, quantitative experimental design to isolate the computational costs of each detection paradigm while holding the underlying generation task, hardware, and decoding configuration constant. The design below reflects the benchmark harness as actually implemented and executed, rather than an idealized, unconstrained configuration.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Variables:} (1) Detection Paradigm (SelfCheckGPT, RAG Verification, LLM-as-a-Judge), (2) Output Sequence Length (short: $<$100 tokens, medium: 100--300 tokens, long: $>$300 tokens), (3) Batch Size (1 for the lightweight configuration; 1 and 4 for the research-paper-scale configuration), and (4) Model Scale (0.5B vs. 1.5B parameters, see below).
    \item \textbf{Dependent Variables (Outcomes):} Time complexity (estimated TTFT proxy in milliseconds, end-to-end latency, throughput in tokens/second) and space complexity (peak VRAM in MB, KV-cache size in MB, vector-store memory in MB), alongside the primary accuracy metrics (precision, recall, F1-score against the gold hallucination labels defined in Section III-C).
    \item \textbf{Models:} Two configurations are reported. A lightweight configuration (\texttt{-{}-lite}) uses Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct as both the generator and the LLM-as-a-Judge judge (the generator's weights are reused for judging, at zero extra VRAM cost) and BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 as the embedding model, with $N=3$ SelfCheckGPT samples. A research-paper-scale configuration (\texttt{-{}-paper}) swaps in Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct for the same generator/judge roles (again weight-shared), keeps BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 as the embedder, and increases SelfCheckGPT's sample budget to $N=5$; a heavier Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct judge can optionally be loaded afterward, once the primary engines are freed, for a second judging pass. Both configurations are reported so that the effect of model scale on the measured cost ordering can itself be checked (Section IV-D).
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Software Environment:} Both reported runs execute on a single free-tier NVIDIA T4 GPU (16GB VRAM, e.g., a Google Colab free-tier instance), using the HuggingFace \texttt{transformers} backend in fp16. The benchmark harness also supports a vLLM backend with native PagedAttention KV-cache introspection for dedicated-GPU deployments (Section III-G), but both reported runs use the \texttt{transformers} backend to fit within free-tier session-time and memory constraints; consequently, the \texttt{transformers} backend processes prompts within a batch sequentially rather than as a single fused GPU batch, and KV-cache is reported as a formula-based estimate (Section III-G) rather than a measurement introspected from vLLM's block manager. This substitution is discussed as an explicit scale/hardware limitation in Section IV-E.
    \item \textbf{Decoding Settings:} Baseline generation uses \texttt{do\_sample=True} with up to 300 new tokens, without a fixed per-call random seed. Because the offline \texttt{generate} call used by the \texttt{transformers} backend does not expose native per-token streaming timestamps, we do not measure true streaming Time-To-First-Token; instead, we report an \emph{Estimated TTFT Proxy} (henceforth ``TTFT proxy''), defined as total generation latency divided by the number of output tokens. This proxy is an approximation of first-token latency and should not be interpreted as a direct streaming measurement. SelfCheckGPT's $N$ additional samples reuse this same sampling configuration on the same prompt. LLM-as-a-Judge and RAG verification are both run against the single, shared baseline generation, so all three paradigms are always judging the same generated text for a given prompt.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset and Gold-Label Construction}

We draw prompts from a stratified sample of 100 items, split evenly between HaluEval (question-answering subset) and TruthfulQA, so that each source contributes 50 prompts (seed$=42$ for both the sampling and the shuffle that interleaves the two sources) and the combined set preserves the intrinsic/extrinsic hallucination split reported in the source datasets. Both datasets are loaded directly from the HuggingFace Hub at run time via the \texttt{datasets} library; because HaluEval has been re-hosted under a couple of different community repository names, the loader tries a short, explicit list of known repo/config/split combinations in order and fails loudly (rather than silently substituting mock data) if none resolve, so that a reported run is never silently backed by synthetic data.

HaluEval and TruthfulQA differ substantially in task structure, annotation methodology, and hallucination prevalence: HaluEval's question-answering subset supplies a knowledge snippet alongside each question and constructs its \texttt{hallucinated\_answer} field by prompting an LLM to fabricate a plausible-but-wrong answer that contradicts that snippet, so its hallucinations are intrinsic and grounded against a known reference; TruthfulQA, by contrast, poses questions deliberately designed to elicit common human misconceptions, and its incorrect answers are extrinsic in the sense that they are not grounded in any snippet provided alongside the question but rather reflect widely held false beliefs. The two datasets were also built with different annotation pipelines (LLM-generated contrastive answers for HaluEval vs. curated human-written misconceptions for TruthfulQA) and, as Section IV-A/IV-D report, produce different positive (hallucination) base rates once passed through our lexical-overlap heuristic (94\%/82\% for HaluEval vs. 50\%/46\% for TruthfulQA at the two model scales). We combine them into a single 100-prompt benchmark, rather than evaluating each in isolation, for three reasons specific to this paper's goal of comparing detection paradigms rather than characterizing any one dataset in isolation: (1) intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations are the two categories Huang et al. \cite{huang2023survey} identify as jointly constituting the hallucination problem (Section II-A), so a benchmark restricted to only one category would understate the generality of any accuracy or cost conclusion; (2) SelfCheckGPT, RAG verification, and LLM-as-a-Judge are, by design, agnostic to whether a hallucination is intrinsic or extrinsic---none of the three detectors receives the source-dataset label as input---so combining the two datasets tests whether each paradigm's relative cost and accuracy ordering holds across both hallucination types rather than being an artifact of one; and (3) the 50/50 stratified split keeps either source from dominating the combined base rate, so the reported aggregate F1/precision/recall figures in Section IV are not simply a restatement of one dataset's own label distribution. The corresponding limitation is that our combined-set accuracy numbers are a weighted average over two dissimilar label distributions and cannot be disaggregated into a single, dataset-independent accuracy estimate; where this matters, Section IV-A and IV-D report the per-source base rates directly so the reader can judge each detector's behavior on the two components separately rather than relying on the pooled figure alone.

The labels shipped with HaluEval and TruthfulQA describe their own reference or best/incorrect answers; they do not directly describe whether a \emph{new} response generated by our own generator model hallucinates. We therefore derive gold labels for the actual object being judged---the generator's own output---as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each of the 100 prompts, the configured generator model (Qwen2.5-0.5B- or Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Section III-B) produces one baseline response under the decoding settings in Section III-B.
    \item Each generated response is tagged intrinsic or extrinsic following the source-dataset heuristic of Huang et al. \cite{huang2023survey}: HaluEval items are treated as intrinsic (their hallucinated samples are constructed against a supplied knowledge snippet), and TruthfulQA items are treated as extrinsic (their incorrect answers are ungrounded, common misconceptions).
    \item A binary Hallucinated / Not Hallucinated ground-truth label is then assigned via a lexical-overlap heuristic: the generated response is labeled Hallucinated if it has greater token-set overlap (Jaccard similarity over whitespace-tokenized, lower-cased text) with the dataset's own \texttt{hallucinated\_answer} field than with its \texttt{reference\_answer} field, and Not Hallucinated otherwise.
\end{enumerate}

These heuristic labels, not the datasets' original reference answers, are the ground truth against which all three detectors are scored. We report this explicitly as a heuristic rather than a human-verified label: it is a pragmatic, fully automatic stand-in for the human annotation or NLI-based labeling a larger-scale study would use, and its known biases (Section IV-E) bound the strength of the accuracy conclusions in Section IV.

\subsection{Retrieval Corpus Construction}

The knowledge source used for RAG verification is a single, fixed, in-memory corpus so that retrieval conditions do not vary across prompts within a run. Rather than an external Wikipedia dump, the corpus is built directly from the reference answers already present in the 100-prompt evaluation set (Section III-C), giving an index of exactly 100 short passages. Each passage is embedded with BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 and indexed in FAISS using an exact, flat inner-product index (\texttt{IndexFlatIP}) over L2-normalized vectors, consistent with the $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d_{embed})$ exact-search complexity described in Section III-A; this is deliberately not an approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) index such as HNSW, IVF, or ScaNN, so no sub-linear retrieval complexity applies at any corpus size, including the $V=100$ passages used here. Because $V$ is small in this benchmark, the absolute cost of exact search is negligible regardless of this distinction, but the distinction would become material at larger corpus sizes, where an ANN index would be the appropriate choice and would change the retrieval-stage complexity term accordingly (Section III-A). Retrieval for each claim returns its top-$k{=}3$ nearest passages by inner-product similarity; the same corpus is used only for RAG verification's own claim-checking step and is not reused to construct the gold labels in Section III-C, which are computed independently via the lexical-overlap heuristic.

\subsection{Prompt Standardization Across Methods}

To ensure that differences in measured accuracy and cost reflect the detection paradigm and not incidental prompt-engineering differences, we fix three things across all three methods: (1) a single generation prompt template used to produce the baseline response for every condition, so SelfCheckGPT's extra samples, the RAG verifier, and the judge all operate on the same underlying generated text; (2) a single instruction template per paradigm (one for SelfCheckGPT's consistency-sampling prompt, one for the judge's verdict prompt), used verbatim across all 100 prompts with no per-item customization; and (3) zero-shot instructions throughout, so no paradigm benefits from a larger or better-tuned few-shot context than another. RAG verification's atomic-claim step uses a regex sentence-boundary split as a lightweight proxy for full claim decomposition (e.g., the LLM-based decomposition used by FActScore \cite{min2023factscore}); this is a simplification we flag explicitly, since a true claim decomposer would change RAG verification's own time complexity by adding an extra decomposition pass.

\subsection{Data Collection Procedure}

Data collection is automated via a custom Python benchmarking harness designed to intercept and log hardware-level metrics at each stage of the detection pipeline. For each of the 100 prompts, the following procedure is executed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Generation:} The generator produces a response under the fixed decoding settings (Section III-B) and fixed prompt template (Section III-E). We record the baseline TTFT proxy and throughput.
    \item \textbf{Detection Execution:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{SelfCheckGPT:} We trigger $N$ additional generations ($N=3$ for \texttt{-{}-lite}, $N=5$ for \texttt{-{}-paper}) using the same decoding settings. We measure the incremental VRAM spike via PyTorch's peak-memory allocator and estimate KV-cache growth analytically from batch size, sequence length, and model architecture (Section III-A's $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot L \cdot d_{model})$ formula), since the \texttt{transformers} backend used for both reported runs does not expose vLLM-style block-manager introspection.
        \item \textit{RAG Verification:} We split the output into sentence-level claims, embed them, and query the fixed FAISS index described in Section III-D. We record the embedding and retrieval latency separately and calculate the vector-store memory footprint as $V \times d_{embed} \times 4$ bytes (FP32 embeddings).
        \item \textit{LLM-as-a-Judge:} We construct the fixed judge prompt and measure the latency and TTFT proxy (Section III-B) of the judge model's forward pass.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric Logging:} Peak VRAM is captured via \texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated()}. All latency measurements are preceded by 1 (\texttt{-{}-lite}) or 3 (\texttt{-{}-paper}) warm-up generations to mitigate cold-start GPU initialization effects. For every row we additionally log the mechanistic quantities that drive each paradigm's cost: the generator's baseline output length in tokens (shared across all three paradigms, since they all judge the same generation); for RAG verification, the number of atomic claims extracted and the number of passages retrieved per claim; for LLM-as-a-Judge, the constructed judge prompt length and the generated verdict length, both in tokens; and for SelfCheckGPT, the realized sample count $N$ and the mean token length of the additional sampled generations. These quantities are reported alongside \texttt{detection\_overhead\_ms} in Section IV so that latency differences between paradigms can be explained mechanistically rather than left as unexplained totals.
    \item \textbf{Failure Handling:} A row is flagged \texttt{failed} when a paradigm's core output is missing or unparseable for that prompt---for LLM-as-a-Judge, a raw verdict that does not parse to a clean Hallucinated/Not-Hallucinated decision; for RAG verification, a response from which no atomic claims could be extracted; for SelfCheckGPT, a missing consistency score. Failed rows are logged with their failure reason, excluded from the accuracy computation in the next step (so they are neither scored as correct nor as incorrect), and their failure rate is reported per paradigm in Section IV alongside the other mechanistic quantities, since a nonzero failure rate is itself a cost of a paradigm that pure F1/precision/recall numbers would otherwise hide.
    \item \textbf{Accuracy Scoring:} Each detector's Hallucinated/Not Hallucinated verdict is compared against the gold labels from Section III-C to compute precision, recall, and F1-score, over the rows that were not flagged as failed in the previous step.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Processing and Analysis Tools}

The entire benchmarking pipeline is built on open-source, industry-standard tools to ensure reproducibility:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inference Engine:} the \texttt{transformers} (HuggingFace) library is used for both reported runs, in fp16 on the T4 GPU described in Section III-B. The harness also implements a \texttt{vLLM} backend with native PagedAttention KV-cache introspection \cite{kwon2023efficient} for deployments with a dedicated GPU, but this backend was not used for the runs reported in Section IV, and KV-cache figures there are therefore analytical estimates (Section III-G) rather than measurements taken from vLLM's block manager.
    \item \textbf{Vector Store:} \texttt{FAISS} (\texttt{IndexFlatIP}) is used for RAG verification indexing and exact nearest-neighbor retrieval over the 100-passage corpus described in Section III-D. Vector-store memory is computed analytically as $V \times d_{embed} \times 4$ bytes rather than measured from FAISS internals.
    \item \textbf{Embedding Model:} \texttt{BGE-small-en-v1.5} is used for claim embedding in both reported configurations, chosen over the larger \texttt{BGE-large-en-v1.5} to keep embedding-model VRAM usage compatible with the free-tier T4 budget shared with the generator/judge model.
    \item \textbf{Data Processing and Statistics:} Raw telemetry data is aggregated using \texttt{Pandas}. Statistical significance of the accuracy-versus-compute trade-offs is evaluated using paired $t$-tests, comparing each pair of paradigms on the same 100-prompt set for a given metric (e.g., SelfCheckGPT vs. LLM-as-a-Judge on \texttt{detection\_overhead\_ms}), and one-way ANOVA when comparing all three paradigms simultaneously on a given metric. Both are computed with \texttt{scipy.stats} and \texttt{scikit-learn} (for precision/recall/F1). Visualizations of the Pareto frontier (F1-score vs. detection overhead) and metric distributions are generated using \texttt{Matplotlib} and \texttt{Seaborn}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From Metrics to Practical Guidelines}

The paper's contribution claims of ``empirical insights'' and ``practical guidelines'' are operationalized, not left implicit. Concretely: (1) for the harness's unit-consistent cost metric, \texttt{detection\_overhead\_ms}, we plot F1-score against that variable for all three paradigms, producing a Pareto frontier that makes dominated configurations (lower accuracy \emph{and} higher cost) visually and statistically identifiable via the tests in Section III-G; (2) from this frontier we derive the decision guidance in Section IV-E, which maps concrete deployment constraints---a fixed latency budget, a fixed VRAM ceiling, and a fixed accuracy floor---to the paradigm that satisfies the constraint at the lowest measured cost; (3) this guidance, together with the raw Pareto and TTFT-proxy-distribution plots produced by the harness, is the deliverable referred to as ``practical guidelines'' in the abstract and introduction. Unlike an idealized design with a held-out confirmation set, the reported guidance is validated directly against the same 100-prompt run it is derived from and is re-checked for stability by repeating the run at a second model scale (Section IV-D); we flag the absence of a separate held-out confirmation set as a limitation in Section IV-E.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Detection Accuracy (Lightweight Configuration)}

Table~\ref{tab:lite-accuracy} summarizes the discriminative capability of the three paradigms within the lightweight configuration. Both RAG verification and SelfCheckGPT achieve comparatively high F1-scores, driven primarily by elevated recall values. However, an analysis of the predicted positive rates suggests that both methods frequently adopt a majority-class prediction strategy, classifying the majority of prompts as hallucinated. Given the high baseline prevalence of hallucinations in the evaluation set, this behavior provides limited discriminative value beyond a naive baseline. Conversely, LLM-as-a-Judge demonstrates high precision but substantially lower recall, leading to a reduced F1-score. This observation suggests that the lightweight judge model may lack the semantic capacity to reliably identify hallucinations without adopting a highly conservative decision boundary.

\subsection{Computational Overhead (Lightweight Configuration)}

The temporal costs associated with each detection mechanism are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:lite-overhead}. The empirical results indicate substantial differences in computational overhead across the evaluated paradigms. RAG verification operates with comparatively low latency, whereas SelfCheckGPT exhibits higher temporal costs due to the necessity of multiple autoregressive generations. This difference reflects the distinction between retrieval-based spatial lookups and generative temporal processing. Table~\ref{tab:lite-mechanistic} decomposes these costs into their underlying mechanistic drivers. The low computational overhead of RAG verification is largely attributable to the execution of nearest-neighbor searches over a constrained vector space, which bypasses the autoregressive bottleneck. In contrast, the latency of LLM-as-a-Judge is primarily determined by the forward pass required to process the concatenated prompt and verdict, while the overhead of SelfCheckGPT scales with the sampling budget $N$.

\subsection{Statistical Significance (Lightweight Configuration)}

To evaluate the observed cost disparities, statistical analyses were conducted on the latency distributions. A one-way ANOVA indicates a significant main effect of the detection paradigm on computational overhead. Subsequent pairwise paired $t$-tests suggest that the latency differences between the evaluated paradigms are statistically significant. Conversely, tests applied to the baseline TTFT and throughput metrics yield no significant variance, providing evidence that the experimental harness effectively separated the detection-specific computational costs from the shared generation workload.

\subsection{Discussion and Recommendation}

The empirical results yield several critical insights regarding the deployment of hallucination detection systems. First, RAG verification achieves a highly favorable position on the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier within the evaluated configuration. By leveraging vector-space retrieval, it attains elevated recall without the autoregressive overhead characteristic of generative methods. However, this advantage relies on the use of a curated, low-noise retrieval corpus and a lightweight claim decomposition heuristic; deployment in open-domain scenarios with extensive, noisy vector stores may alter this trade-off.

Second, the performance of LLM-as-a-Judge is sensitive to model capacity and prompt calibration. While the lightweight judge exhibited high precision and lower recall, scaling to a dedicated 3B model improved accuracy at the expense of increased latency and parsing constraints. This suggests that LLM-as-a-Judge is generally more suited for asynchronous, high-stakes verification tasks where latency constraints are relaxed, rather than real-time streaming applications.

Third, the statistical analyses provide evidence that the computational disparities between paradigms are substantial and stable across model scales. The reliance of SelfCheckGPT on multiple autoregressive sampling passes results in elevated latency and increased KV-cache utilization, making it challenging to deploy in latency-sensitive production environments without further algorithmic optimization.

Fourth, the evaluation highlights the impact of ground-truth label distributions on perceived accuracy. The lexical-overlap heuristic utilized to generate gold labels tends to favor high-recall methods, given the base rate of hallucinations in the combined dataset. Practitioners should consider the baseline prevalence of errors in their specific deployment domains when interpreting these aggregate metrics.

Fifth, the reliance on the \texttt{transformers} backend for these evaluations provides a baseline estimate of computational overhead. Production environments utilizing optimized inference engines, such as vLLM with PagedAttention \cite{kwon2023efficient}, may exhibit different absolute memory footprints, particularly regarding KV-cache management during concurrent sampling.

Finally, the non-zero failure rate observed in LLM-as-a-Judge verdict parsing introduces an additional operational consideration. Deployment architectures utilizing generative judges may require robust fallback mechanisms or structured output constraints to ensure system reliability. Ultimately, for real-time applications with constrained memory and latency budgets, RAG verification presents a highly viable paradigm, provided the retrieval corpus can be effectively bounded and indexed.

\section{Conclusion}

This study presented a controlled comparative benchmark evaluating the computational and accuracy trade-offs of three prominent LLM hallucination detection paradigms: SelfCheckGPT, RAG verification, and LLM-as-a-Judge. By treating computational overhead as a primary evaluation metric, the empirical results indicate that these methods exhibit distinct temporal and spatial complexity profiles. RAG verification consistently occupies a favorable region of the accuracy-versus-cost Pareto frontier, achieving competitive F1-scores at latencies substantially lower than generative alternatives. Conversely, while LLM-as-a-Judge offers high precision, its recall limitations and autoregressive latency constrain its utility in real-time scenarios unless augmented with larger, more computationally demanding judge models. The reliance of SelfCheckGPT on repeated sampling introduces elevated latency and KV-cache overhead, limiting its practical viability in highly constrained environments.

The practical implications of these findings offer an empirically grounded decision framework for system architects. For latency-sensitive deployments with access to curated knowledge bases, RAG verification represents a highly effective strategy. Future work should extend this benchmarking methodology to encompass open-domain, noisy retrieval corpora, evaluate the impact of approximate nearest-neighbor indexing on RAG latency, and investigate the efficacy of structured output constraints in mitigating the parsing constraints associated with LLM-as-a-Judge paradigms.
